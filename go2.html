Build and Implement

Open the models crowd scientists use: measure crowds from data, then build macroscopic, cellular-automaton, social-force and evacuation models, and test them against data.

Is this go for you?

You need: Go 1, or its exit check. Basic Python: variables, functions, loops and lists. NumPy helps but is not required.

How it works: Every module starts from a connections map, explains the mechanism with a worked calculation, and ends with a small model you build and run in Python, here in the browser.

Quick entry check

1. A macroscopic crowd model describes…

Macroscopic models track density and flow over space and time; microscopic models track each person.

2. In a floor-field cellular automaton, people…

Each step, every person picks a neighbouring cell, with a preference set by the floor fields.

3. Required safe egress time (RSET) is…

RSET is the occupants' time; ASET is the time available. A safe design keeps RSET below ASET with a margin.

All three right? You could skip ahead to Go 3, or use this go as revision. Not sure? Revisit Go 1.

What you will produce

  • Small working models in Python: a fitted fundamental diagram, a 1-D macroscopic flow, a floor-field cellular automaton, a social-force corridor and an evacuation calculator
  • Density, speed and flow measured from trajectory data
  • A calibration note comparing a model with the fundamental diagram and a bottleneck experiment

The path

  1. 2.1Measuring crowds: counts, trajectories and densityEvery model starts from measurements. Learn to get density, speed and flow from counts and trajectories, and why the Voronoi method gives smoother, fairer densities than counting heads in a box.45 min
    Data
  2. 2.2Macroscopic models: crowds as a fluidTreat the crowd as a continuous density, conserve people, and let the fundamental diagram decide the flow. A jam forms at a bottleneck and its tail travels backwards, exactly as the numbers predict.45 min
    Model
  3. 2.3Cellular automata and floor fieldsDivide the floor into 40 cm cells, one person per cell. Each step, people hop to a neighbouring cell, guided by a static field (distance to the exit) and a dynamic field (others' trails).45 min
    Model
  4. 2.4The social force modelEach person is pulled towards a goal and pushed by neighbours and walls, like particles on springs. The model reproduces lanes, clogging at doors and 'faster is slower', but needs careful parameters.50 min
    Model
  5. 2.5The crowd model family: choosing the right toolHydraulic calculations, macroscopic, cellular, force-based, velocity-based, step-based and heuristic models each answer different questions. Choose by the question, the scale and the data you have.40 min
    Compare
  6. 2.6Route and exit choiceWhere people go matters as much as how they walk. Shortest-path, quickest-path and equilibrium rules give different crowd patterns; good guidance moves the crowd towards the best split.40 min
    Behaviour
  7. 2.7Evacuation modelling: RSET and ASETAn evacuation is safe when the time people need (RSET) is less than the time available (ASET), with a margin. Pre-movement time is usually the biggest and most uncertain part.45 min
    Safety
  8. 2.8Calibration, validation and uncertaintyA model is only as trustworthy as the data it was tested against. Calibrate parameters on one data set, validate on another, test standard cases, and report the uncertainty.45 min
    Method

An open course on crowd dynamics and crowd safety, taught in three goes, with mass gatherings as the research case. Facts follow the cited literature and guidance (Fruin, Weidmann, Helbing and colleagues, Seyfried and colleagues, Still, and the Green Guide, among others). Explorers and toy models are teaching versions with illustrative numbers: never use them on their own to size or approve a real venue.

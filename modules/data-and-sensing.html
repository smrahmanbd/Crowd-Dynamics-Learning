Spoken overview
Read the transcript

How many people are here, and how dense is it? For a mass gathering, these are hard questions. The oldest method is Herbert Jacobs': divide the area into zones, estimate the density of each, multiply and add. It is simple, but only as good as the densities you assume, and reported crowd sizes are often wildly exaggerated. Modern methods use computer vision. Neural networks trained on annotated images learn to produce a density map, a picture in which the brightness of each pixel is the expected number of heads there, so the total count is the sum of the map. The multi-column network of Zhang and colleagues and the ShanghaiTech dataset made this approach popular. Cameras need calibration for perspective, and very dense crowds, where heads overlap, remain hard. Drones give overhead views; phone data, from mobile networks or Wi-Fi, give coverage over large areas, as in analyses of Itaewon. All these methods have errors, so report intervals, not single numbers. And all of them raise privacy questions that must be answered before the data are collected.

كم عدد الناس هنا، وما مدى الكثافة؟ في التجمعات الحاشدة هذه أسئلة صعبة. وأقدم طريقة هي طريقة هربرت جيكوبس: قسّم المساحة إلى مناطق، وقدّر كثافة كل منها، ثم اضرب واجمع. إنها بسيطة، لكنها لا تكون أفضل من الكثافات التي تفترضها، وكثيراً ما تكون أحجام الحشود المعلنة مبالغاً فيها بشدة. وتستخدم الطرق الحديثة الرؤية الحاسوبية. فالشبكات العصبية المدرّبة على صور مشروحة تتعلم إنتاج خريطة كثافة، أي صورة يكون فيها سطوع كل بكسل هو العدد المتوقع للرؤوس هناك، فيكون العدد الكلي مجموع الخريطة. وقد جعلت الشبكة متعددة الأعمدة لجانغ وزملائه ومجموعة بيانات شنغهاي تِك هذا النهج شائعاً. وتحتاج الكاميرات إلى معايرة للمنظور، وتبقى الحشود الكثيفة جداً، حيث تتداخل الرؤوس، صعبة. وتعطي الطائرات المسيّرة منظراً علوياً؛ وتعطي بيانات الهواتف، من شبكات الاتصال أو الواي فاي، تغطية لمساحات واسعة، كما في تحليلات إيتايوون. ولكل هذه الطرق أخطاء، فأبلغ عن فترات لا عن أرقام مفردة. وكلها تثير أسئلة خصوصية يجب الإجابة عنها قبل جمع البيانات.

Jacobs' method and its uncertainty

N ≈ Σzones areaz × densityz

Example: 12,000 m² at 3 per m², 18,000 m² at 1.6 and 30,000 m² at 0.5 give about 79,800 people. If each density is uncertain (say ± 0.5, 0.3 and 0.15), a Monte Carlo gives a 90% interval of roughly 64,000 to 95,000. The largest share of the uncertainty comes from the zone with the largest area × uncertainty. Densities above about 4 per m² across whole zones are rare, so very high reported totals deserve scepticism.

Density maps from images

Instead of detecting each person, a neural network learns to output a density map: each pixel's value is the expected number of heads there, so the sum is the count. Zhang et al. (2016) introduced a multi-column network and the ShanghaiTech dataset; many later models followed. Challenges: perspective (people far away are tiny), occlusion in very dense crowds, lighting, and transfer to new cameras and cultures (clothing such as the white ihram garments of pilgrims differs from training data).

Training data

A counting model trained on street scenes may undercount dense crowds in white clothing under strong sun. Validate on your own site.

Overhead views and phone data

Drones and high cameras give near-overhead views, ideal for density and trajectory extraction, but need permits and safety rules over crowds. Phone data (mobile network records, Wi-Fi and Bluetooth probes) estimate presence and movement over large areas; the Itaewon analysis used mobile network data to estimate visitor numbers. They are biased by phone ownership and settings and are highly privacy-sensitive.

Privacy by design

Crowd data are personal data. Use data minimisation (count, do not identify), aggregation (density per cell, not tracks of named people), short retention, secure storage and clear purposes, and follow the applicable law and ethics approvals. Publish methods and error estimates, not raw footage.

Research notes

  • Jacobs' method: area × density per zone; report an interval.
  • Neural networks count crowds by predicting density maps; perspective and occlusion are hard.
  • Drones and phone data add coverage with their own biases.
  • Crowd data are personal data: minimise, aggregate and protect.

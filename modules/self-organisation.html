Self-organisation: lanes, oscillations and waves

Without a leader, crowds form lanes in counterflow, take turns at narrow doors, and make stop-and-go waves at high density. Simple local rules create these patterns.

Spoken overview
Read the transcript

Watch a busy station corridor where people walk in both directions. Nobody gives orders, yet within seconds the crowd sorts itself into lanes: people heading the same way follow each other, because walking behind someone going your way means fewer collisions. At a narrow door with people going both ways, small groups pass in turns, like traffic lights nobody installed. In very dense crowds, movement becomes stop-and-go: waves of stopping travel backwards through the crowd, like the ripples in a traffic jam on a motorway. These patterns are called self-organisation. They come from simple, local rules: avoid the person in front, follow someone going your way, step into a gap. Some patterns help, like lanes, which raise flow. Others are warnings: stop-and-go waves mean the crowd is near or above capacity. In this module you will see these patterns emerge in a small simulation, and learn to read them as signals.

Lanes in counterflow

In a two-way corridor, people who follow someone walking their way meet fewer oncoming walkers. This small advantage spreads: soon the corridor carries lanes of uniform direction. Lane formation is one of the most robust findings in crowd dynamics and appears in experiments and in models such as the social force model (Helbing and Molnár 1995).

Look for it

Next time you walk through a busy station or market, watch for lanes. Count how many there are across the corridor.

Taking turns at doors

At a narrow door with people going both ways, one side wins for a while, then pressure on the other side grows and the direction flips. The result is oscillation: alternating bursts. One-way doors, or separate in and out doors, remove the conflict.

Stop-and-go waves

When density is near capacity, one person slowing down makes the person behind slow down a little more, and so on. The disturbance grows and travels backwards through the crowd as a stop-and-go wave. Helbing, Johansson and Al-Abideen (2007) filmed such waves at the Jamarat Bridge before the 2006 disaster, followed by crowd turbulence (module 1.7).

Like traffic

Stop-and-go waves in crowds behave much like phantom jams on motorways: no accident needed, just density near capacity.

Reading patterns as signals

Patterns are information. Lanes mean the crowd is coping. Stop-and-go means it is near capacity. Irregular, random-looking surges mean it is far beyond safe density. Control-room staff learn to watch for these changes on cameras, often before any count is available.

Words and links

New words

Self-organisation
Large-scale patterns that arise from local interactions without central control.
Lane formation
Sorting of a two-way flow into lanes of uniform direction.
Oscillation
Alternating passage of opposite flows through a narrow opening.
Stop-and-go wave
A wave of stopping that travels backwards through a dense crowd.

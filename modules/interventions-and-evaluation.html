Interventions and their evaluation

Research brief

In this module you will

  • describe common crowd interventions and how they work
  • show with a model how metering moves waiting from one place to another
  • design an evaluation of an intervention with a fair comparison
  • look for unintended effects
Spoken overview
Read the transcript

Once you understand where risk builds up, you can change the system. The most common tools are simple. Metering limits the rate at which people enter a space, so waiting happens in a safer place, often an open approach rather than an enclosed plaza. One-way systems separate opposing flows, as on the rebuilt Jamarat Bridge. Holding areas store people safely and release them in batches. Scheduling spreads arrivals over time. Information tells people where to go and when, and stewards make it real. But interventions do not make risk disappear; they move it. Metering creates a queue outside, which must itself be safe. A one-way system lengthens some routes. A holding area can become a crush if it is not released in time. That is why every intervention needs an evaluation: compare it fairly with a baseline, in simulation first and with data after, and look for unintended effects. In this module you will meter the gates in front of a plaza and see density inside fall while the queue outside grows.

The toolbox

Metering: admit people at a controlled rate. One-way systems: separate opposing flows. Holding areas: store and release in batches. Scheduling: spread demand over time. Information and stewarding: guide choices and enforce plans. Design changes: widen bottlenecks, remove obstacles, add routes. Most real plans combine several.

Metering moves the queue

A 1,200 m² plaza feeds a venue at 180 people per minute, with arrivals peaking at 320 per minute. Without metering the plaza peaks at about 2.5 per m². Metering the gates at 200 per minute keeps it under 1 per m², but a queue of over 2,000 builds outside (lab, 20 random seeds). Metering is only an improvement if the space outside is open, wide and managed.

Evaluating fairly

Compare the intervention with a baseline under the same demand, using the same model and outputs, over several seeds and sensitivity cases. Report where risk exposure goes, not only whether it falls at the target. After implementation, monitor with data and compare with predictions. Real-world before–after comparisons must allow for other changes, such as different attendance.

Unintended effects

Look for: queues in unsafe places; longer routes that people refuse to follow (short-cuts through barriers); holding areas that fill faster than planned; counterflows created by people going back for friends; and information overload. People adapt to interventions, so simulate behavioural responses (for example non-compliance) as part of the sensitivity analysis.

Research angle

Few interventions at mass gatherings have been evaluated with before–after data. Designing such evaluations is a valuable research contribution.

Research notes

  • Interventions: metering, one-way systems, holding areas, scheduling, information, design changes.
  • Interventions move risk as well as reduce it; metering moves waiting upstream.
  • Evaluate against a fair baseline and report where risk exposure goes.
  • Include people's adaptation, such as non-compliance and short-cuts, in tests.

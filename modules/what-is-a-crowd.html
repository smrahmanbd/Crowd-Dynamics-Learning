What a crowd is, and why it matters

A crowd is many people sharing one space and often one purpose. Its behaviour comes from simple local rules, and its risks from too many people in too little space.

Spoken overview
Read the transcript

A crowd is a large number of people in one place at the same time, often sharing a purpose: watching a match, catching a train, or performing a pilgrimage. Crowds are usually safe, friendly and even pleasant. But they follow a simple physics. Every person needs space, and every route can carry only so many people each minute. When more people arrive than a space or a gate can pass, they wait, and the waiting crowd grows like water filling a bath. Most deadly crowd incidents were not caused by people panicking. They happened because too many people were packed into too little space, so that pressure built up and people could no longer move or breathe. In this course you will learn to see a crowd three ways: as people with goals, as a flow through space, and as a system that designers and managers can keep safe. We start with the big picture: what a crowd is, the three phases of an event, and why the numbers matter.

A crowd, in plain words

A crowd is a large number of people in one place at the same time. Some crowds share a purpose (a concert audience, pilgrims, commuters); others are just people passing (a busy street). Crowd scientists care about crowds because most of the time they are safe and enjoyable, and occasionally they are deadly, and the difference is usually predictable from numbers.

We describe a crowd with three linked quantities: density (people per square metre), speed (metres per second) and flow (people passing a line per second).

Three phases of every event

Every event has three phases: ingress (arriving), circulation (moving around inside) and egress (leaving). Each has its own peak. Egress is often the most intense, because everyone leaves at once at the end.

Crowd safety expert Keith Still built these phases into his DIM-ICE model, which you will meet in module 1.8.

A queue is a stock

Picture a bath. The tap is the arrival rate, the plug hole is the gate's capacity, and the water level is the number of people waiting. If the tap runs faster than the plug drains, the level rises. It keeps rising until arrivals fall below capacity, so the biggest queue comes after the busiest arrival time.

change in people waiting = arrivals − departures

A worked example: 90 people a minute arrive at a gate that passes 70. The queue grows by 20 people every minute.

Try it in your head

A stadium gate passes 60 people a minute. For 30 minutes, 100 people a minute arrive. How many are waiting at the end? (100 − 60) × 30 = 1,200.

Crowd crushes are not panic

News reports often say a crowd 'panicked' or 'stampeded'. Research tells a different story. In most deadly incidents, people were packed so tightly that they could not move, breathe or escape: compressive asphyxia. People in danger mostly help each other (Drury, Cocking and Reicher 2009).

Examples you will meet again: the Hillsborough stadium disaster (1989, 97 deaths), the Love Parade in Duisburg (2010, 21 deaths), the Mina crush during the Hajj (2015, 769 deaths in the official Saudi count; independent counts exceed 2,000) and the Itaewon crowd crush in Seoul (2022, 159 deaths). In each, too many people reached too little space.

The word 'stampede'

'Stampede' suggests animals running wildly and blames the victims. Most crushes happen where people are barely moving. Prefer 'crowd crush' or 'crowd collapse'.

Remember

  • A crowd is described by density, speed and flow.
  • Every event has ingress, circulation and egress phases, each with its own peak.
  • A waiting crowd is a stock: it grows while arrivals exceed capacity, and peaks after the arrival peak.
  • Deadly crowd incidents are about density and pressure, not panic.

Check yourself

1. A gate passes 50 people per minute and 80 arrive per minute for 10 minutes. How many are waiting at the end?

(80 − 50) × 10 = 300 people.

2. Which event phase is often the most intense?

At the end everyone leaves at once, so egress flows are often the largest.

3. What killed most victims of major crowd crushes?

People were squeezed so tightly they could not breathe.

4. The biggest queue usually forms…

The queue keeps growing until arrivals fall below capacity, which is after the peak.

Words and links

New words

Crowd
A large number of people gathered in one place at one time, often sharing a purpose.
Ingress
The arrival phase of an event.
Circulation
Movement within a venue during an event.
Egress
The departure phase of an event, normal or emergency.
Compressive asphyxia
Inability to breathe because the chest is squeezed, the main cause of death in crowd crushes.
Stock
A quantity that accumulates, such as the number of people waiting.

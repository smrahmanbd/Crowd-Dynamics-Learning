Spoken overview
Read the transcript

From far away, a dense crowd looks like a fluid. Macroscopic models use that view. Instead of tracking every person, they track density along a corridor or across a square. The basic rule is that people are conserved: the change in the number of people in a stretch of corridor equals those coming in minus those going out. To close the equations we need the fundamental diagram, which tells us the flow for each density. A simple, robust way to compute this is to cut the corridor into cells. Each step, the flow from one cell to the next is the smaller of two numbers: what the upstream cell can send, called demand, and what the downstream cell can receive, called supply. When the corridor narrows, the bottleneck's supply is smaller, a jam forms in front of it, and the tail of the jam moves backwards, just as you saw in Go 1. Roger Hughes extended the idea to two dimensions, with people moving down the fastest route to their goal. Macroscopic models are fast, so they suit large networks and quick what-if questions.

People are conserved

Cut a corridor into cells of length Δx. Over a time step Δt, the number of people in cell i changes by what flows in minus what flows out:

ρi(t + Δt) = ρi(t) + (Δt ÷ Δx) × (fi−1→i − fi→i+1)

This is the discrete form of the continuity equation ∂ρ/∂t + ∂(ρv)/∂x = 0, the basis of the LWR model (Lighthill, Whitham and Richards), first written for road traffic.

Demand, supply and the flow between cells

Which flow goes between cells? The cell transmission (Godunov) rule says: the smaller of what the upstream cell can send (demand) and what the downstream cell can receive (supply). Below the critical density a cell can send J(ρ) and receive up to capacity; above it, it can send capacity but receive only J(ρ).

f = min( D(ρup), S(ρdown) )

Keep Δt small enough that nobody crosses more than one cell per step (v0Δt ≤ Δx).

Same maths as roads

The cell transmission model (Daganzo 1994) is used for road traffic too. Crowds only need a different fundamental diagram.

A jam at a bottleneck

Make cells 40 to 45 m half as wide. Their supply is halved, so less can enter. Density in front rises past critical: a jam. Its tail moves backwards at a speed set by the fundamental diagram (the slope between the upstream and jammed states). With an inflow of 0.9 per m per s into a bottleneck that passes only about 0.61, the queue tail retreats by about 5 to 6 m every minute in the lab model.

Explore it

Set the bottleneck share to 1 (no bottleneck): does any jam form at an inflow of 0.9? Then lower it step by step.

Two dimensions and where macro models shine

Hughes (2002) wrote a 2-D continuum model in which people move in the direction that minimises their travel time to the goal, taking into account the density ahead. Macroscopic models are fast and smooth, so they suit large networks, many scenarios and real-time forecasting. They cannot show individual choices, groups or pushing at a door: for those, use microscopic models (modules 2.3 to 2.5).

Remember

  • Macroscopic models treat the crowd as a density field and conserve people.
  • The flow between cells is the minimum of upstream demand and downstream supply.
  • At a bottleneck a jam forms and its tail travels backwards.
  • Macro models are fast and suit large networks; they cannot show individual behaviour.

Words and links

New words

Macroscopic model
A model that describes a crowd by continuous density and flow fields.
Continuity equation
∂ρ/∂t + ∂(ρv)/∂x = 0: conservation of people.
Demand (sending function)
The flow a cell can send downstream.
Supply (receiving function)
The flow a cell can accept from upstream.
Cell transmission model
A discrete macroscopic model using min(demand, supply) between cells.
Hughes model
A 2-D continuum crowd model where people follow the fastest route.

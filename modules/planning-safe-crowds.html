Spoken overview
Read the transcript

How do organisers keep crowds safe? Crowd scientist Keith Still teaches two tools. The first, DIM-ICE, crosses three things organisers control, design, information and management, with the three phases of an event, ingress, circulation and egress. Each of the nine cells is a question: is the design of the entry route adequate, does information reach people during circulation, how is egress managed? The second tool, RAMP, looks at routes, areas, movement over time, and the profile of the crowd. Then come numbers. Guidance for sports grounds in the United Kingdom, the Green Guide, gives planning flow rates of eighty two people per metre width per minute on level routes and sixty six on stepped routes, and egress times of up to eight minutes. Divide the number of people by the flow rate and the time, and you get the exit width needed. Finally, practical measures keep density down: separate flows, one-way systems, holding areas, staggered times, and stewards with clear authority to act.

DIM-ICE

Keith Still's DIM-ICE model crosses what organisers control with the phases of an event:

IngressCirculationEgress
Designentry routes, gatesconcourses, toiletsexits, routes away
Informationtickets, signsscreens, announcementsexit signs, PA
Managementqueues, searchesstewardingstaggered release

Each cell is a question to ask. Clusters of weak cells point to where an incident is likely.

RAMP analysis

RAMP asks about Routes (where people come from and go), Areas (holding and moving spaces, measured), Movement (arrival and departure profiles over time) and Profile (who the crowd is: age, culture, familiarity, purpose). A football crowd, a family festival and a pilgrimage need different plans even in the same space.

Sizing exits with flow rates

The Green Guide (6th edition) gives planning flow rates of 82 people per metre width per minute on level routes and 66 on stepped routes, and egress times of up to 8 minutes (less where fire risk is higher).

exit width = people ÷ (flow rate × egress time)

Example: 6,000 people, level route, 8 minutes: 6,000 ÷ (82 × 8) ≈ 9.1 m. Stepped route: 6,000 ÷ (66 × 8) ≈ 11.4 m. Note these are per minute, unlike the per-second specific flows of module 1.4.

Rates are not laws of nature

Planning rates include safety margins and apply to the venues they were written for. Use the guidance that applies where you work, and check routes beyond the exit too.

Keeping density down on the day

Good plans fail without good operations. Measures that work: separate in and out flows; one-way systems; holding areas and metering to release people in batches; staggered start and finish times; clear information in the crowd's languages; and stewards with the authority to stop inflow when density rises.

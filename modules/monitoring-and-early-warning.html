Research brief

In this module you will

  • compute density and crowd-pressure maps from video-derived data
  • design warning and action levels tied to specific responses
  • explain lead time and why early indicators matter
  • describe how forecasts and digital twins support decisions
Spoken overview
Read the transcript

The best time to stop a crush is long before it happens. Control rooms at large events watch cameras and sensors and look for signs that density is rising towards critical levels. Video analysis can turn camera images into maps of density and local velocity. From these, crowd pressure, the local density times the variance of velocity, highlights places where a dense crowd has begun to move irregularly, which in the Jamarat data came before falls. A monitoring system needs levels: a watch level, at which staff look more closely, and an action level, at which a predefined response begins, such as stopping inflow, opening a relief route or making an announcement. Each level must be tied to an action and a person with the authority to take it. Lead time matters: a warning is only useful if there is time to act. Short-term forecasts, from models fed by live counts, and so-called digital twins of the venue can extend that lead time. In this module you will build a crowd-pressure map and set warning levels on it.

From video to maps

Split the scene into cells of about 1 m². In each cell and time window, estimate density (from a counting network or tracking) and local velocity (from optical flow or tracks). Then compute indicators per cell:

Pcell = ρcell × Var(Vcell) over the last few seconds

Smooth over time to avoid false alarms from single frames.

Levels tied to actions

A useful scheme has at least two levels: watch (look closely, prepare) and act (start the predefined response). In the lab, with a watch level of 0.01 and an action level of 0.02 per s², a synthetic dense zone near a bottleneck reaches 0.066, with 20 of 240 cells above the action level. Thresholds must be set from data and guidance for the site, tested in exercises, and reviewed after events. A level without an action, or an action without someone authorised to take it, is useless.

Lead time and forecasts

At Jamarat in 2006, stop-and-go began about 30 minutes, and turbulence about 10 minutes, before the first deaths (Helbing et al. 2007, as reported in the press). That is the window for action. Short-term forecasts extend it: a macroscopic or network model fed with live gate counts can predict density on a route 10 to 30 minutes ahead. A digital twin is such a model kept continuously in step with the site.

Pitfalls

False alarms erode trust; missed alarms cost lives. Camera coverage is never complete. Density estimates degrade in very dense crowds, at night and in rain. Operators can be overloaded with screens. At Itaewon, emergency calls warned of danger hours before the crush, but no effective action followed: monitoring only works when it is tied to response.

Open question

How to set and validate warning thresholds across different sites and cameras is an open research problem.

Research notes

  • Turn video into density and velocity maps per cell, smoothed in time.
  • Indicators such as crowd pressure cross predefined watch and action levels.
  • Each level needs a defined action and someone with authority to take it.
  • Forecasts and digital twins extend lead time; monitoring without response fails.

New words

Optical flow
A computer-vision estimate of motion between video frames.
Watch level
A threshold at which operators increase attention and prepare.
Action level
A threshold that triggers a predefined response.
Lead time
Time between a warning and the onset of danger.
Digital twin
A model of a site kept continuously updated with live data.

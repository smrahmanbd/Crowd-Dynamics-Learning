Research brief

In this module you will

  • write a researchable question about crowd safety at a mass gathering
  • choose outcome measures such as risk exposure, not just peak density
  • build a scenario design matrix and count the runs it needs
  • plan data, ethics and stakeholder involvement
Spoken overview
Read the transcript

Mass gatherings bring together very large numbers of people for a common purpose: religious pilgrimages such as the Hajj, festivals, sports tournaments, concerts and large celebrations. They are hard to study. You cannot run experiments on two million pilgrims, data are scarce and sensitive, and the stakes are high. So the research question must be sharp. Instead of asking whether the event is safe, ask something you can answer: how much does staggering group departures reduce the time spent above four people per square metre on a given route, compared with the current schedule? Next, choose outcome measures. Peak density is easy to report but hides how long and how many people were exposed. Risk exposure, the person-minutes spent above a density threshold, captures both. Then design your scenarios: demand levels, schedules, layouts, management measures, and the combinations between them. Plan your data, from counts and video to schedules and site plans, and how you will calibrate and validate. Finally, plan for ethics: privacy of video, consent, and the involvement of the authorities and communities affected.

What makes mass gatherings special

Mass gatherings concentrate very large numbers of people in space and time. The Hajj brings close to two million pilgrims to Makkah and the holy sites of Mina, Muzdalifah and Arafat in a few days, following a fixed ritual sequence. Many pilgrims are older, travel in national groups and speak dozens of languages. Heat, health and crowding interact (Memish et al. 2012). Similar challenges, at smaller scale, arise at festivals, tournaments and city celebrations.

A researchable question

Use a structure like PICO: Population (pilgrims on route X), Intervention (staggered departures), Comparison (current schedule), Outcome (person-minutes above 4 per m²). A good question names the place, the change, the comparison and the measure, and can be answered with the data you can get.

Example questions

Does gate metering at the plaza move risk exposure to safer open approaches? How sensitive is the peak density on a route to 20% of groups leaving early? Which of three layouts minimises counterflow conflicts?

Outcome measures that matter

Peak density alone is misleading: a short spike and an hour at the same level differ hugely in risk. Report time above thresholds and risk exposure:

exposure = Σt ρ(t) × A × Δt   for ρ(t) above the threshold (person-minutes)

In the lab, a synthetic density curve peaking at 6.5 per m² on a 600 m² ramp spends 29 minutes above 4 per m², giving about 96,000 person-minutes of exposure.

Scenario design and ethics

List the factors (demand, schedule, layout, management) and their levels. A full factorial design of 2 × 2 × 3 × 2 levels gives 24 scenarios; with 10 random seeds, 240 runs. One-factor-at-a-time designs need fewer runs but miss interactions. Plan ethics early: video and phone data are personal data, consent is often impractical at mass gatherings, so anonymisation, data minimisation and approval from the responsible authorities are essential.

Too many scenarios

Twelve factors at three levels give over half a million combinations. Screen factors first, then study the important ones in depth.

Research notes

  • Mass gatherings are large, synchronised and diverse; experiments are rarely possible.
  • Frame a specific, answerable question (population, intervention, comparison, outcome).
  • Report risk exposure and time above thresholds, not only peak density.
  • Design scenarios to capture interactions, and plan data ethics from the start.

Reading list

New words

Mass gathering
An event concentrating very large numbers of people at a place and time, such as the Hajj or a festival.
Risk exposure
Person-minutes spent above a density threshold.
Full factorial design
A scenario design containing every combination of factor levels.
PICO
Population, Intervention, Comparison, Outcome: a structure for research questions.
Data minimisation
Collecting only the personal data needed for the purpose.

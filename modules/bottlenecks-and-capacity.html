Spoken overview
Read the transcript

Think of a wide corridor ending in a single door. However wide the corridor, everyone must squeeze through that door. The narrowest point on a route is the bottleneck, and its capacity sets the flow for the whole route. A good first estimate is simple: capacity equals the width of the bottleneck times a specific flow, typically between about one point three and one point nine people per metre per second in laboratory experiments. If people arrive faster than that, a queue forms in front of the bottleneck and grows by the difference every second. Inside a bottleneck, people often interleave like the teeth of a zipper, using the space efficiently. In this module you will find bottlenecks, calculate their capacity, and estimate how long a crowd takes to pass through. You will also see why a bottleneck in the wrong place, such as a narrowing at the bottom of a slope, can be dangerous.

Find the bottleneck

Walk the route from start to finish and look for the narrowest effective width: a door, a turnstile line, a stair, a gate in a fence, a street narrowed by stalls. The route can carry no more than that point allows. Upgrading anything else does not help.

Capacity from width

capacity = width × specific flow   (people per second)

Laboratory experiments found specific flows of about 1.3 to 1.9 people per metre per second through bottlenecks (Seyfried et al. 2009). A 2 m door at 1.3 per m per s passes 2.6 people per second, or 156 per minute: 2,000 people take about 13 minutes.

Guidance for stadiums uses planning rates per minute instead, such as 82 people per metre per minute on level routes in the Green Guide (module 1.8).

Your turn

A 1.2 m exit at 1.3 per m per s. How long for 500 people? Capacity 1.56 per s, so 500 ÷ 1.56 ≈ 320 s, about 5.3 minutes.

How queues grow

If 3.5 people arrive per second at that 2 m door, the queue grows by 3.5 − 2.6 = 0.9 people per second: 540 people after 10 minutes. The queue packs at high density right in front of the door, often 3 to 5 per m² or more.

Zippers, steps and bad places

Inside bottlenecks people form overlapping lanes, the zipper effect. Some experiments suggested capacity rises in steps as each new lane fits (Hoogendoorn and Daamen 2005); others found nearly linear growth (Seyfried et al. 2009). For design, linear growth is a reasonable first estimate.

Where a bottleneck sits matters: a narrowing at the end of a downhill slope, at a T-junction, or where two flows meet concentrates pressure. The Itaewon alley in 2022 was narrow, sloping and fed from both ends.

Doubling the width

Doubling width roughly doubles capacity only if the space before and after the bottleneck can supply and absorb the extra flow.

Remember

  • The bottleneck, the narrowest point, sets a route's capacity.
  • Capacity ≈ width × specific flow (about 1.3 to 1.9 per m per s in experiments).
  • Queues grow at demand minus capacity, and pack densely in front of the bottleneck.
  • Where a bottleneck sits (slopes, junctions, meeting flows) can matter as much as its width.

Words and links

New words

Bottleneck
The narrowest point on a route; it limits the route's flow.
Effective width
The width people can use, often less than the clear width because people keep away from walls.
Zipper effect
Overlapping lanes formed by people passing through a bottleneck.
Demand
The rate at which people want to pass a point.

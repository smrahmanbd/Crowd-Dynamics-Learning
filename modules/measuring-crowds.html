Spoken overview
Read the transcript

Before you can model a crowd, you have to measure it. The richest data are trajectories: the position of every person, many times a second, extracted from video. Researchers at Forschungszentrum Jülich and partners have run hundreds of laboratory experiments and published the trajectories openly. From trajectories you can compute the three basic quantities. Density can be measured the classical way, by counting heads in a measurement area and dividing by its area, but that number jumps every time someone steps in or out. The Voronoi method gives each person the patch of floor closer to them than to anyone else; density inside the area is then the average of one over each patch's size, weighted by how much of the patch lies inside. It is smoother and responds to small changes. Speed comes from the change in position over time, and flow from counting people crossing a line. In this module you will compute all three from synthetic trajectories, and check that flow equals density times speed.

Trajectories

The richest crowd data are trajectories: (x, y) positions of each person at each time. They come from overhead video processed by tracking software such as PeTrack, or from experiments where participants wear coloured caps. The Jülich pedestrian dynamics data archive publishes hundreds of such experiments.

Classical and Voronoi density

Classical density counts people in a measurement area A: ρ = N ÷ A. It jumps by 1 ÷ A each time someone enters or leaves. Voronoi density (Steffen and Seyfried 2010) gives each person i the region closer to them than to anyone else (area Ai) and averages:

ρV = (1 ÷ A) × Σi (area of cell i inside the box) ÷ Ai

It varies smoothly, works in small areas, and reduces scatter in fundamental diagrams.

Speed and flow

Speed is distance moved over a short time: v = Δx ÷ Δt, usually over 0.5 to 1 s to smooth out the sway of each step. Flow is the number of people crossing a line in a time window, divided by the window, and by the width for specific flow. In a steady crowd, the three must agree: J ≈ ρ × v. If they do not, check the measurement area, time window or tracking.

Edge effects

Voronoi cells at the edge of the crowd are unbounded. Cut them at a sensible distance or with walls, as PedPy does.

Beyond the laboratory

Real events rarely have overhead cameras everywhere. Practitioners combine counts (gates, turnstiles, ticket scans), CCTV with computer-vision counting (module 3.4), and phone-based data such as Wi-Fi probes or network data. Each has errors and privacy issues: report both.

Remember

  • Trajectories give density, speed and flow; the Jülich archive and PedPy make them accessible.
  • Classical density jumps; Voronoi density is smoother and works in small areas.
  • J ≈ ρ × v is a built-in check on your measurements.
  • Field data combine counts, CCTV and phone data, each with errors and privacy issues.

Trajectory
The path of one person: their position at successive times.
Measurement area
The region in which density and speed are measured.
Voronoi density
Density from each person's Voronoi cell: smoother than counting heads.
Voronoi cell
The region of floor closer to one person than to any other.
PedPy
Open-source Python library for pedestrian data analysis.

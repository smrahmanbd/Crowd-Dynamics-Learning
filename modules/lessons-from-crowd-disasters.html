Spoken overview
Read the transcript

Crowd disasters are rare, but each one teaches lessons at a terrible price. At Hillsborough in Sheffield in nineteen eighty-nine, a gate was opened to relieve a crush outside the stadium, and supporters flowed into central pens that were already full; ninety-seven people died. At the Love Parade in Duisburg in two thousand and ten, a single ramp served as the only entrance and exit of the festival area; inflow and outflow met, density soared, and twenty-one people died. At Mina during the Hajj in two thousand and fifteen, two large streams of pilgrims met where streets two hundred and four and two hundred and twenty-three join; the official Saudi count was seven hundred and sixty-nine deaths, and independent counts exceeded two thousand. At Itaewon in Seoul in two thousand and twenty-two, a narrow sloping alley filled from both ends during Halloween, with no event plan; one hundred and fifty-nine people died. Researchers such as Helbing and Mukerji describe such events as systemic failures. In this module you analyse them with DIM-ICE, separating the proximate cause, the crush itself, from the underlying causes in design, information and management.

Proximate and underlying causes

The proximate cause of death in crowd disasters is usually compressive asphyxia in a crush or crowd collapse. The underlying causes are the decisions and conditions that allowed density to reach that level: design, information and management. Investigations that stop at the proximate cause, or blame 'panic', miss what can be fixed.

Four cases

Hillsborough (1989): the Hillsborough Independent Panel (2012) documented how a gate was opened to relieve congestion outside, and supporters entered a tunnel into central pens that were already full. Love Parade (2010): Helbing and Mukerji (2012) reconstructed from video how inflow and outflow met on the ramp that was the festival's only access. Mina (2015): two streams met at a junction; official and independent death counts differ widely, a reminder that even basic facts can be contested. Itaewon (2022): an unorganised Halloween gathering in narrow streets; the alley was about 3 to 4 m wide, sloped and fed from both ends, and emergency calls began hours before the crush.

Analysing with DIM-ICE

Place each failure in the DIM-ICE matrix (module 1.8). At the Love Parade, weaknesses cluster in design × ingress/egress (a single ramp) and management × circulation (handling the ramp during the event). At Itaewon, almost every cell is weak, because there was no organiser: design, information and management were all missing. Clusters of weak cells show where the system failed.

Evidence and hindsight

Use primary sources: official inquiries, court records, published video analyses, and peer-reviewed reconstructions. Beware hindsight bias: what is obvious after the event was rarely obvious before it, to people with partial information. Good research asks what decision makers knew, when, and what better information or design would have changed.

Systemic failure

Helbing and Mukerji (2012) argued that the Love Parade disaster resulted from many interacting factors rather than one mistake: a systemic failure.

Research notes

  • Proximate cause: crush and asphyxia; underlying causes: design, information and management.
  • Hillsborough, Love Parade, Mina and Itaewon all involved flows meeting or accumulating in confined spaces.
  • DIM-ICE locates failures by phase and shows clusters.
  • Crowd disasters are systemic failures; analyse them with primary evidence and without hindsight bias.

Reading list

New words

Proximate cause
The immediate cause of harm, such as compressive asphyxia.
Underlying cause
The conditions and decisions that made the harm possible.
Systemic failure
A disaster resulting from many interacting weaknesses rather than one error.
Hindsight bias
Seeing past events as more predictable than they were.

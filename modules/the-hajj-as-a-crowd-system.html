Research brief

In this module you will

  • describe the Hajj ritual sequence as a set of synchronised crowd movements
  • explain how the Jamarat Bridge was redesigned after 2006
  • explain time-slot scheduling of pilgrim groups and its sensitivity to compliance
  • identify research questions for Hajj crowd management
Spoken overview
Read the transcript

The Hajj is one of the largest regular mass gatherings in the world. In recent years close to two million pilgrims have performed it, moving together between Makkah and the holy sites of Mina, Arafat and Muzdalifah over a few days, following a ritual sequence fixed by religion. That synchronisation is what makes crowd management so demanding: everyone needs to be in the same places at nearly the same times. The stoning ritual at the Jamarat in Mina has been the most critical point. After the disaster of January two thousand and six, studied by Helbing and colleagues, the Jamarat Bridge was rebuilt as a multi-storey structure with separate levels, wide ramps and one-way flows, and the authorities introduced schedules that assign pilgrim groups to time slots. A metro line now links the holy sites. In this module you will see the Hajj as a crowd system, and explore how scheduling spreads demand over time, and how quickly its benefits vanish when groups ignore their slots.

A synchronised crowd

The rites take place on specific days at specific places, so demand is synchronised rather than spread out as at a city festival. The movement from Arafat to Muzdalifah after sunset on the day of Arafat, and the stoning ritual at the Jamarat in the following days, concentrate very large flows on a few routes.

Redesigning the Jamarat

Helbing, Johansson and Al-Abideen (2007) analysed video of the old Jamarat Bridge entrance before the January 2006 disaster and identified stop-and-go waves and crowd turbulence (module 1.7). Their recommendations fed into the redevelopment: the new Jamarat is a multi-storey structure with several levels, wide access ramps and one-way flows, so that streams to and from the pillars do not meet. Johansson et al. (2008) describe the measures used during the first Hajj after the disaster, which passed without a major incident at the bridge.

From data to design

The Jamarat redesign is a rare example of video-based crowd research feeding directly into infrastructure and operations.

Scheduling groups

Because pilgrims travel in organised groups, the authorities can assign time slots for movements such as the stoning ritual. If 450,000 pilgrims use a route that carries 60,000 people per hour (toy numbers), six evenly spread slots give 75,000 per slot: still too many in one hour, so slots must be split further or routes added. The lab's greedy schedule of 60 groups into 12 slots keeps the peak below 47,000 per hour, but if 12 groups arrive in the first slot instead of their own, the peak jumps to about 166,000.

Open research questions

How robust are schedules to non-compliance and delays? How should real-time monitoring adjust release times? How do heat and health interact with crowd density and walking speed for older pilgrims? How can data from cameras and phones be used while protecting privacy? These are live questions for Hajj research, and transfer to other mass gatherings.

Research notes

  • Hajj demand is synchronised by the ritual sequence and concentrated on a few routes.
  • After 2006 the Jamarat was rebuilt with several levels, wide ramps and one-way flows.
  • Time-slot scheduling of groups spreads demand, but its benefit depends on compliance.
  • Open questions: robustness, real-time adjustment, heat and age, and privacy.

New words

Hajj
The annual Islamic pilgrimage to Makkah and the holy sites of Mina, Arafat and Muzdalifah.
Jamarat
The pillars in Mina where pilgrims perform the stoning ritual, and the multi-level bridge built around them.
Time-slot scheduling
Assigning groups to times for a movement to spread demand.
Compliance
The share of groups that keep to their assigned slots or routes.

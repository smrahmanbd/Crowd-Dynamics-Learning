Spoken overview
Read the transcript

You now know three ways to model a crowd: as a fluid, as people hopping on a grid, and as particles pushed by forces. There are more. Velocity-based models choose a speed and direction directly, for example the collision-free speed model, where each person walks as fast as possible without colliding within a short time horizon. The optimal steps model, developed by Seitz and Köster, moves people in discrete steps, choosing the best point on a circle around them. Cognitive heuristic models, from Moussaïd, Helbing and Theraulaz, assume people look ahead and head where they can walk furthest towards their goal without a collision. At the other end of the scale, the hydraulic method is a hand calculation with flow rates and walking speeds, still used in building codes. No model is best for everything. The question decides: sizing an exit, forecasting a city-wide event, studying pushing at a door, or exploring how individual decisions shape a crowd. Open simulators such as JuPedSim and Vadere implement several of these models, so you can compare them on the same scenario.

Seven families

From simplest to most detailed: hydraulic calculations; macroscopic (continuum and cell-transmission) models; cellular automata; force-based models; velocity-based models; optimal steps; cognitive heuristics. The model atlas has an animation, a simulator and a mind map for each.

Velocity-based and step-based models

The collision-free speed model (Tordeux, Chraibi and Seyfried 2016) sets each person's speed to the largest value that avoids a collision within a time gap T:

v = min( v0, max(0, (s − ℓ) ÷ T) )

where s is the distance to the person ahead and ℓ the body length. It reproduces the fundamental diagram with few parameters. The optimal steps model (Seitz and Köster 2012) moves people in steps to the best point on a circle, scoring distance to the goal and personal space.

Cognitive heuristics

Moussaïd, Helbing and Theraulaz (2011) proposed two rules: (1) walk in the direction that gets you closest to your goal before you would collide with someone, scanning your field of vision; (2) walk at a speed that keeps a time gap τ to the first obstacle. Adding body contact forces, the same rules reproduce lanes, stop-and-go waves and turbulence at high density.

Choosing by the question

Ask: what scale (a door, a building, a city)? Which phenomenon (capacity, queues, pushing, behaviour)? Which data can calibrate it? How will decision makers understand it? For a code check, a hydraulic calculation may be right; for a city festival network, a macroscopic or CA model; for pushing at a gate, a force-based model; for how information changes behaviour, a cognitive or agent-based model with route choice.

Words and links

New words

Hydraulic method
Egress calculation using flow rates and speeds, without individual behaviour.
Velocity-based model
A model in which agents choose their velocity directly, usually to avoid collisions.
Collision-free speed model
Speed = min(v₀, (gap − body length)/T) (Tordeux, Chraibi and Seyfried 2016).
Optimal steps model
Agents move in discrete steps to the best point on a circle (Seitz and Köster 2012).
Cognitive heuristics
Simple perception-based rules for direction and speed (Moussaïd et al. 2011).

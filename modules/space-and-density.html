Read the transcript

How full is a space? Crowd scientists answer with one number: density, the number of people per square metre. Count the people in an area and divide by the area. Turn it upside down and you get the space each person has. At one person per square metre you can walk freely. At two, you have to adjust your speed and path. At four, you are shuffling and brushing against others. At six or more, you are pressed by bodies on all sides and cannot move on your own. In the nineteen seventies the engineer John Fruin turned these experiences into levels of service, graded from A, free movement, to F, where flow breaks down. Planners still use them to design walkways, platforms and queues. In this module you will measure density, read levels of service, and estimate how many people a space can hold comfortably. Remember: the same thousand people are comfortable in a large square and dangerous in a narrow street.

Density, the key number

Density is the number of people per square metre, written ρ (rho). Count the people in a known area and divide:

ρ = number of people ÷ area (people per m²)

Example: 120 people in a 60 m² lobby give ρ = 2 people per m². The space per person is the inverse: 60 ÷ 120 = 0.5 m² each.

Quick check

A platform is 100 m long and 4 m wide, and 600 people wait on it. Density? 600 ÷ 400 = 1.5 per m², about 0.67 m² each: level E for a walkway, tolerable for waiting.

What density feels like

Rough guide, for adults moving in one direction: below 1 per m² you walk freely; about 2 you adjust speed and path; about 4 you shuffle with frequent contact; above about 5 to 6 you cannot move independently and pressure can build (Fruin 1993; Still 2014). Standing still, people tolerate higher densities than walking, which is why queues and viewing areas use different thresholds.

Levels of service

John Fruin (1971) graded walkways A to F by space per person, like grades on a report card. Designers choose a target grade for the busiest 15 minutes, often C or D for stations and B or C for comfortable public spaces. Separate scales exist for queuing areas and stairs, because standing and climbing need different space.

How many people fit?

Run the calculation backwards. A 2,000 m² square, of which 1,600 m² is usable, at a comfortable 2 people per m² holds 3,200 people. At 4 per m² it holds 6,400, but people can no longer move freely. Remember that density is never even: people gather near stages, exits and screens, so average density hides dangerous hot spots.

Average density

'The square was only half full' can still hide 6 per m² at the front. Always ask where the people were, not just how many.

Remember

  • Density ρ = people ÷ area; space per person = 1 ÷ ρ.
  • Below about 1 per m² people walk freely; above about 5–6 per m² they cannot move independently.
  • Fruin's levels of service grade space per person from A to F.
  • Holding capacity = usable area × accepted density, but averages hide hot spots.

Check yourself

1. 200 people stand in a 50 m² area. What is the density?

200 ÷ 50 = 4 people per m².

2. At 2 people per m², how much space does each person have?

Space per person = 1 ÷ 2 = 0.5 m².

3. Fruin's level of service F means…

F is under about 0.5 m² per person, where movement breaks down.

4. Why can an average density be misleading?

Crowds are uneven; danger is local.

Words and links

New words

Density
Number of people per square metre (ρ).
Space per person
Area available to each person, the inverse of density, in m².
Level of service
A grade (A to F) of crowd comfort based on space per person or flow (Fruin 1971).
Holding capacity
The number of people an area can hold at an accepted density.
Usable area
The area people can actually occupy, excluding obstacles.

Spoken overview
Read the transcript

Most crowds are safe. Danger comes when density gets very high. Above about five or six people per square metre, nobody can move independently: you are carried by the crowd. Forces from many people pushing, even gently, add up through bodies and can reach levels that make breathing impossible. Researchers Dirk Helbing, Anders Johansson and Habib Zein Al-Abideen studied video of the 2006 Jamarat Bridge disaster during the Hajj. They saw the crowd change from smooth flow to stop-and-go waves, and then to crowd turbulence: irregular, random-looking surges that pushed people several metres and made them fall. They measured crowd pressure, density times the variance of local speeds, and found it rose sharply before people fell. Once one person falls in such a crowd, others fall over them, and a crowd collapse spreads. The lesson from Hillsborough, Love Parade, Mina and Itaewon is the same: keep density well below critical levels, watch for warning signs, and never let large flows meet in narrow spaces.

Risk rises steeply with density

Below about 2 people per m² risk is low. Around 4 to 5 per m² people are in frequent contact. Above about 5 to 6 per m² they cannot move independently, and forces from others can be transmitted through them (Fruin 1993; Still 2014). Densities of 7 to 9 per m² or more have been measured in fatal incidents.

Crowd pressure and turbulence

Helbing, Johansson and Al-Abideen (2007) analysed video of the Jamarat Bridge entrance before the January 2006 disaster. Flow changed from laminar to stop-and-go, then to crowd turbulence. They defined crowd pressure as:

P = ρ × Var(V)   (density × variance of local velocity)

In their data, falls and deaths followed when P rose above about 0.02 per s². Pressure is high only when the crowd is both dense and moving irregularly, which makes it a useful warning signal.

One number is not a law

The 0.02 s⁻² value comes from one site and one camera set-up. It is a research finding, not a design standard.

Four disasters, one lesson

Hillsborough, Sheffield (1989): 97 deaths after a crowd was let into already full central pens. Love Parade, Duisburg (2010): 21 deaths on a ramp that was the only way in and out. Mina, during the Hajj (2015): two large flows met at a junction of streets 204 and 223; the official Saudi toll was 769, independent counts exceed 2,000. Itaewon, Seoul (2022): 159 deaths in a sloping alley about 3 to 4 m wide, fed from both ends with no event plan.

In each case, flows were allowed to meet or accumulate in a confined space faster than it could empty.

Intervene early

The chain has many links, and the early ones are easiest to break. Before the event: design routes so flows do not meet head-on, size spaces for the peak, plan for the unexpected. During the event: watch density and warning signs, stop or meter inflow early, open relief routes, and tell people at the back what is happening.

Remember

  • Above about 5–6 per m² people lose control of their movement.
  • Crowd pressure = density × variance of local velocity; high values preceded falls at Jamarat in 2006.
  • Crushes follow a chain: demand above capacity, high density, forces, turbulence, falls.
  • The disasters at Hillsborough, Love Parade, Mina and Itaewon share one cause: flows meeting or accumulating in confined spaces.

Words and links

New words

Crowd pressure
Local density × variance of local velocity; a warning indicator (Helbing et al. 2007).
Crowd turbulence
Irregular, random-looking motion of a very dense crowd that displaces people and causes falls.
Crowd collapse
Progressive falling of people in a dense crowd.
Crowd crush
An incident in which people are compressed so much that they are injured or killed.

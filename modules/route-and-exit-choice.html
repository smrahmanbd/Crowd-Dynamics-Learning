Spoken overview
Read the transcript

Imagine two exits: one near, one far. If everybody takes the nearest, it queues while the far one stands empty. If some take the far exit, they walk longer but wait less, and everyone gets out sooner. The best split equalises the time at which each exit finishes. Models capture this with different rules. The simplest is the shortest path: walk the fewest metres. A better rule is the quickest path: choose the route with the least expected time, walking plus waiting. If everyone chooses selfishly in this way, the crowd reaches a user equilibrium, where no one could do better by switching. The system optimum, best for everyone together, can be different, and may need guidance to reach. In practice, people also follow habit, signs and others, as we saw in Go 1. Simulators compute route choice with distance fields, for example the fast marching method, and update them as congestion grows. In this module you will find the best split between two exits and see how a simple expected-time rule can find it on its own.

Shortest path and distance fields

The simplest rule: everyone takes the shortest path. In simulators this is a distance field, the distance from every point to the exit, computed for example with the fast marching method, which handles obstacles. People walk down its gradient. With several exits, each person heads for the nearest, which can overload one exit badly.

Quickest path and the best split

Add waiting. A person's expected time to exit k is walking time plus the queue ahead divided by capacity:

tk = dk ÷ v + Qk ÷ ck

Example: 2,000 people, exit A at 30 m, exit B at 90 m, each passing 4 per s, speed 1.2 m/s. All to A: about 8.8 minutes. Best split: about 45% to B, everyone out in about 5.0 minutes. A simple rule where each person picks the exit with the smaller expected time finds the same split.

User equilibrium and system optimum

When everyone minimises their own time, the crowd reaches a user equilibrium: all used routes take the same time and no one gains by switching. The system optimum minimises a total, such as the time until the last person leaves or the sum of everyone's delay. In networks the two can differ (as in Braess's paradox, where adding a route can make everyone slower). Guidance aims to close the gap.

Research angle

How far real crowds are from the system optimum, and how much guidance can close the gap, is an active research question.

Guidance in practice

Real people do not compute expected times. They follow habit, signs, stewards and each other. Guidance that works is early, visible and trusted: dynamic signs showing open exits, stewards redirecting at decision points, and apps or announcements with clear instructions. At mass gatherings, routes are often assigned rather than chosen, by schedule and zone (module 3.3).

Remember

  • Shortest-path choice can overload the nearest exit.
  • Quickest-path choice adds expected waiting: t = d/v + Q/c.
  • User equilibrium (selfish) and system optimum (best for all) can differ.
  • Guidance must be early, visible and trusted; mass gatherings often assign routes.

Words and links

New words

Distance field
The distance from every point to a target, used to steer simulated people.
Fast marching method
An algorithm to compute distance (or travel-time) fields around obstacles.
Quickest path
The route with the least expected travel time including waiting.
User equilibrium
A state where no one can reduce their own travel time by changing route.
System optimum
The routing that minimises a total measure for everyone.

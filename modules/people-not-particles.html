People, not particles: behaviour and choice

People choose routes, walk in groups, follow familiar paths and respond to information. Crowd behaviour is social, and 'panic' is rare. Good management works with that behaviour.

Spoken overview
Read the transcript

Physics explains a lot about crowds, but people are not particles. They have goals, habits and friends. When people leave a building, many head for the door they came in by, even when another exit is closer and empty. Families and friends walk together, and a group moves at the pace of its slowest member. People look at what others do and follow them. In emergencies, research shows that people mostly stay calm and help each other, partly because they come to feel part of a shared group. Real problems come from missing or confusing information, delays in deciding to move, and designs that send everyone the same way. That is why good crowd management is not only about widths and capacities. It is about signs that people understand, announcements they trust, stewards they can ask, and routes that match what people naturally do. In this module you will explore how behaviour changes evacuation times, and why the myth of mass panic is so persistent and so misleading.

Familiar routes

Studies of evacuations and exit choice show that many people head for the exit they know, often the one they entered by, even if another is nearer and free. Sime (1985) called this affiliation with familiar places and people. The result: one exit queues while another stands empty.

evacuation time ≈ time of the slowest exit to empty

Groups

At events, most people come with family or friends. Groups move together, walk more slowly, and form walking shapes such as a line abreast or a V (Moussaïd et al. 2010). In evacuations, people look for their group before leaving, which adds to pre-movement time.

The myth of panic

Films and headlines show crowds 'panicking'. Research on real emergencies finds the opposite: people are mostly orderly and helpful, and selfish behaviour is rare (Drury, Cocking and Reicher 2009). What looks like panic in a crush is people being pushed by forces they cannot resist. Blaming panic draws attention away from design, information and management.

Information changes movement

People decide using what they see and hear. Clear signs in the crowd's languages, announcements from trusted voices, and visible stewards shorten decision times and balance routes. Poor information, or none, is a cause in many incidents: crowds keep pushing forward because those at the back cannot see what is happening at the front.

Pilgrims and information

At the Hajj, pilgrims come from over 150 countries and speak many languages. Group leaders, colour-coded signs and staggered schedules are part of how information reaches them.

Remember

  • People often choose familiar routes, which leaves some exits underused.
  • Groups move together and slow evacuations.
  • Mass panic is a myth; cooperation is the norm, and crushes are physical.
  • Clear, early, trusted information changes how crowds move.

Words and links

New words

Affiliation
The tendency to move towards familiar people and places (Sime 1985).
Pre-movement time
Time between an alarm and the start of purposeful movement.
Social identity
A sense of belonging to a group, which can grow among strangers in an emergency.
Mass panic
The myth that crowds in danger turn irrational and selfish.

Fruin (1971); Predtechenskii and Milinskii (1978); Green Guide; SFPE Handbook

Hydraulic (hand calculation)

ScaleWhole venue, by hand
SpaceRoutes as widths
TimeSteady flow
Best forFirst sizing of exits, regulation checks
Key inputsSpecific flow, width, walking speed
SoftwareSpreadsheet; codes and guides

How it moves people (animation)

Discussion

The hydraulic method treats a crowd like water in pipes: each metre of exit width passes a fixed number of people per minute, and walking speed sets the travel time. Egress time is the number of people divided by flow rate times width, plus the time to walk to the exit.

Strengths: fast, transparent, embedded in guidance (82 per m per min on level routes and 66 on stepped routes in the Green Guide). Limits: no behaviour, no route choice, no local density peaks, and it assumes everyone moves at once and every exit is used as planned.

Use it first, to set the scale of the problem and check any simulation result.

Hughes (2002); Lighthill–Whitham–Richards for pedestrians; network models

Macroscopic (continuum)

ScaleLarge areas and networks
SpaceContinuous density field or network links
TimeContinuous or fine steps
Best forLarge sites, timetables, real-time forecasting
Key inputsFundamental diagram, demand, route rule
SoftwareResearch codes; network tools

How it moves people (animation)

Discussion

Macroscopic models describe density and flow rather than individuals. Conservation of people plus a fundamental diagram gives waves of density that move through space, as in traffic flow; Hughes added a route rule in which people move down the gradient of travel time.

Strengths: fast enough for whole sites and timetables, and for forecasting with live data. Limits: no individual behaviour, groups or detailed geometry, and results depend heavily on the fundamental diagram used.

At mass gatherings they cover the network; micro models then zoom in on hot spots.

Burstedde, Klauck, Schadschneider and Zittartz (2001); Kirchner and Schadschneider (2002)

Cellular automata

ScaleRooms to buildings
SpaceGrid of 0.4 m cells
TimeDiscrete steps (~0.3 s)
Best forFast large-scale evacuation studies
Key inputsStatic and dynamic floor fields, coupling kS, kD
SoftwareMany research codes; some commercial tools

How it moves people (animation)

Discussion

The floor is a grid of cells about 0.4 m wide, each empty or holding one person. At each step people move to a neighbouring cell with probabilities set by a static floor field (distance to the exit) and a dynamic field (traces left by others, like ants' pheromones).

Strengths: very fast, simple to explain, reproduce lanes and clogging. Limits: grid artefacts, discrete speeds, and exit flows that can be too high unless calibrated (the Go 2 lab adds an exit friction parameter).

Good for large evacuation studies where speed matters more than fine detail.

Helbing and Molnár (1995); Helbing, Farkas and Vicsek (2000)

Social force model

ScaleRooms, corridors, bottlenecks
SpaceContinuous
TimeSmall time steps (~0.01 s)
Best forDense crowds, contact, faster-is-slower
Key inputsDesired speed, relaxation time, repulsion strengths
SoftwareResearch codes; JuPedSim; many tools

How it moves people (animation)

Discussion

Each person accelerates towards a desired velocity and is pushed by social forces from neighbours and walls, plus physical contact forces at high density. Newton's law moves everyone forward in small time steps.

Strengths: reproduces lanes, oscillations at doors, clogging and faster-is-slower; handles contact at high density. Limits: many parameters, small time steps, and unrealistic oscillations or overlaps if poorly tuned.

The most famous crowd model; use it when contact and pushing matter.

Optimal steps model

ScaleRooms to stations
SpaceContinuous, step positions on a circle
TimeEvent-driven steps
Best forRealistic stepping, stairs, mixed speeds
Key inputsStep length, utility function, personal space
SoftwareVadere (open source)

How it moves people (animation)

Discussion

People move in discrete steps. Each step lands on the point of a circle around the person that best balances getting closer to the goal against keeping personal space from others and walls.

Strengths: natural stepping, no overlaps, fewer oscillations than forces, works with different speeds and stairs. Limits: computationally heavier than CA, and the utility function needs calibration.

Developed in the Vadere framework at Munich University of Applied Sciences.

Tordeux, Chraibi and Seyfried (2016); JuPedSim

Collision-free speed model

ScaleRooms to large venues
SpaceContinuous
TimeSteps of ~0.01–0.05 s
Best forFast, overlap-free simulation of queues and bottlenecks
Key inputsTime gap T, free speed, body size
SoftwareJuPedSim (open source)

How it moves people (animation)

Discussion

A first-order model: each person chooses a direction and a speed. The speed is the largest that keeps a safe time gap to the person in front, so people never collide.

Strengths: few parameters, fast, stable, no overlaps; good at queues and bottlenecks. Limits: no contact forces, so pushing in very dense crowds is not represented.

The default model in JuPedSim; the Go 3 gate lab uses it.

Moussaïd, Helbing and Theraulaz (2011)

Cognitive heuristics

ScaleRooms to streets
SpaceContinuous, vision-based
TimeSmall steps
Best forNatural avoidance, crowd turbulence studies
Key inputsVision field, horizon, relaxation time
SoftwareResearch codes

How it moves people (animation)

Discussion

People scan their field of vision and choose the direction that lets them walk furthest towards the goal without a collision, then adjust speed to keep a safe time to collision. Contact forces are added at high density.

Strengths: grounded in perception, reproduces natural avoidance and, with contact forces, crowd turbulence. Limits: heavier computation, less widely available in software.

A bridge between physics-style models and how people actually see and decide.

Quiz: who am I?

1. "I need only a flow rate per metre of width and a walking speed, and I give you an egress time by hand." Who am I?

It is Hydraulic (hand calculation).

2. "I treat the crowd as a fluid of density, and people move down the gradient of travel time to the exit." Who am I?

It is Macroscopic (continuum).

3. "People in me hop from cell to cell, attracted by a floor field that remembers where others walked." Who am I?

It is Cellular automata.

4. "Every person in me feels a pull towards a goal and pushes from walls and neighbours, like springs." Who am I?

It is Social force model.

5. "I take discrete steps on a circle around each person, choosing the step with the best utility." Who am I?

It is Optimal steps model.

6. "I choose a speed so that I never collide with the person in front, within a time horizon." Who am I?

It is Collision-free speed model.

7. "I scan my field of view and head where I can walk furthest without a collision, then keep a safe time gap." Who am I?

It is Cognitive heuristics.
